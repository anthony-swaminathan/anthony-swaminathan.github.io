This paper investigates the role of non-Ricardian beliefs about future tax incidence in mediating the aggregate economic response to an unfinanced tax cut. Using a standard one-asset HANK model with an endogenous fiscal rule, I simulate a ten-year tax cut under systematically biased beliefs. Behavioral agents forecast their own future incidence as a fixed multiple of the FIRE benchmark and attribute offsetting incidence to households far from them in income and asset space, so that beliefs remain consistent with the government's long-run budget constraint. Systematic optimism operates like a large wealth effect, generating lower labor supply and savings relative to FIRE. Rising interest rates lead to lower investment and a revaluation of existing government debt, worsening the fiscal impact of the tax cut. Output and welfare decrease. Systematic pessimism reverses these signs. Simulations of the Reagan, Bush, and Trump tax cuts under optimism produce results consistent with the muted aggregate expansions following these policies. A decomposition shows that the effect of biased beliefs is highly sensitive to the existing stock of government debt, highlighting the importance of the revaluation channel.
