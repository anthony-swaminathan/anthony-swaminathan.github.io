This paper investigates the impact of ambiguity aversion on worker search behavior, job tenure, and markdowns. I embed ambiguity aversion in a standard Burdett–Mortensen wage-posting model with endogenous search effort, heterogeneous firms, and imperfectly observable amenities. Workers receive repeated signals about the amenity at their current job, but only one signal about each external offer. They are averse to ambiguity over the difference in amenities between their current job and potential outside offers. This aversion leads to stricter acceptance criteria for outside offers, reducing the value of on-the-job search. Workers search less and move less often. The combination of high early returns to learning and long-run selection matches the empirical hump-shaped relationship between quit rates and tenure. Lower worker search and offer acceptance rates weaken the connection between wages and the recruitment and retention margins, leading to a downward shift in the equilibrium offer distribution and an increase in markdowns. Compared to full information and Bayesian learning benchmarks, ambiguity aversion lowers search, wages, productivity, and both worker and aggregate welfare, while raising markdowns, average tenure, and firm welfare.
