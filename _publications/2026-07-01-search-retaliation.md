This paper studies employer retaliation against on-the-job search as a source of endogenous labor market power. I nest a strategic retaliation choice in a Burdett–Mortensen wage-posting model with endogenous search, heterogeneous firms and workers, and unobservable experience-good amenities. Because amenities are unobservable, firms do not know which workers are searching. Instead, on-the-job search generates noisy evidence, with false positives coming from non-searchers. Firms choose the evidentiary threshold for imposing a proportional reduction in wages and productivity, subject to a trade-off between higher retention from reduced search and lower recruitment from reduced job values. In the calibrated model, retaliation appears in equilibrium as the optimal choice of high-productivity firms, covering just over half of the workforce. Search decreases both at retaliating firms and at non-retaliating firms, as the returns to search at the bottom of the job ladder decline. This loosens the connection between wages and the recruitment and retention margins, leading the endogenous wage offer distribution to shift down and markdowns to rise, especially at retaliators. Stationary wages, productivity, and worker and aggregate welfare all decline, while firm value increases. Retaliation thus allows firms to generate endogenous labor market power by modifying workers' search incentives.
