What is the optimal local industry composition when agglomeration raises productivity but specialization concentrates exposure to sectoral shocks? In this paper, I study this question using a dynamic model that treats local industry composition as a portfolio with endogenous returns. Agglomeration effects generate increasing returns to scale, while stochastic industry productivity generates risk. Captive local demand determines how industry shocks pass through to local income, wages, and prices. I estimate the model using U.S. metro-industry data and solve the full 376-metro, 18-industry planner's problem and decentralized equilibrium with neural policy functions. The resulting optimal allocation shows that risk meaningfully constrains optimal specialization. Raising relative risk aversion decreases the wedge between the planner's optimal allocation and the empirical allocation. Higher risk aversion also implies a smaller wedge generated by externalities between the planner and the decentralized equilibrium. Nevertheless, the welfare gain from optimal policy rises, as more risk-averse preferences increase the welfare cost of consumption losses in bad states. Consequently, standard deterministic models of agglomeration that omit sectoral risk can recommend too much specialization and understate the welfare consequences of suboptimal allocations.
