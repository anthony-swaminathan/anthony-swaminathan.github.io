What is the optimal local industry composition when specialization increases expected productivity but also concentrates exposure to sectoral shocks? I study this question using a dynamic model that treats local industry composition as a portfolio with endogenous returns. Agglomeration externalities generate increasing returns to scale, stochastic industry productivity generates risk, and captive local demand determines how shocks pass through the local economy. These features generate a mean-variance trade-off: optimal ex ante specialization depends on both the expected gains from agglomeration and the costs of concentrated sectoral risk. I estimate the model with U.S. metro-industry data and use neural networks to solve the planner's problem and decentralized equilibrium for the full set of U.S. MSAs and two-digit NAICS industries. The resulting optimal allocations show that risk reduces the optimal level of ex ante specialization at plausible levels of relative risk aversion. Higher risk aversion narrows the wedge between the planner's frictionless target and the empirical allocation, while also implying a smaller externality-induced wedge between the planner's and decentralized equilibrium's targets. Nevertheless, the welfare gains from optimal policy rise with risk aversion, as more concave preferences make consumption losses in bad states more costly. These results imply that standard models of agglomeration that omit sectoral risk may overstate the optimal level of specialization and understate the welfare gains from optimal policy.
